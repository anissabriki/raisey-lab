**Title:** Why I Created Raisey Lab

**Standfirst:** A new approach to digital presence in aesthetic medicine.

For years, I worked on the other side of the screen.

At Google, as an Account Manager, I helped brands understand how digital acquisition could become a real business lever: how the right strategy could reach the right audiences and make every investment work harder. It was where I first understood something that has stayed with me ever since.

> Digital is not simply about being online. Done well, it accelerates business.

I then moved into agency life, building international strategies for luxury brands. The scale changed, the budgets changed, [the markets changed](/insights/international-aesthetic-medicine-culture/). But one lesson grew sharper: performance alone is never enough. People don't simply click. They perceive, they compare, they decide whom to trust, and only then do they choose. Brand, image, content, experience and acquisition are not separate subjects. They are parts of the same journey.

Then I entered the world of aesthetic medicine.

#### An industry I became fascinated by

Aesthetic medicine sits at an unusual intersection of medicine, beauty, science, identity and desire. It is driven by innovation, yet surrounded by regulation, shifting cultural perceptions and subjects that can still feel deeply personal, sometimes even taboo.

Having spent years around beauty and luxury, I understood straight away how much image matters here. What I hadn't expected was everything that sits behind the image. Behind every clinic is expertise. Behind every doctor is a point of view: a technique, an aesthetic philosophy, a way of treating a face, a particular relationship with ageing, proportion and natural results.

Online, that difference is often invisible.

#### Choosing a doctor has become a journey

Choosing an aesthetic doctor is rarely a single decision. Someone hears a name from a friend. She searches for it, reads the website, looks at Instagram, listens to how the doctor talks about aesthetics, and compares. Somewhere along the way she begins to answer a more important question: *is this the right person for me?*

More and more, that question is [answered before any consultation](/insights/aesthetic-medicine-patient-journey/), a shift I look at more closely in *Your Patient Has Already Met You*. It means digital presence is no longer just a marketing channel. It has become part of how trust is built.

#### Different patients, different philosophies

Aesthetic medicine is changing too. [Its vocabulary now reaches beyond transformation](/insights/aesthetic-medicine-skin-quality-language/) towards natural-looking results, skin quality, prevention and longevity. Patients respond to different philosophies. One wants subtle rejuvenation. Another is curious about regenerative approaches. Another is looking for a doctor known for a specific technique. Another simply wants to know she will still look like herself.

A clinic cannot speak to all of them in the same way. Its positioning, its editorial choices and its digital experience all shape the opinion someone forms long before she becomes a patient.

#### Visibility is not vanity

For a long time, reputation and word of mouth were enough to build a successful practice. They remain invaluable. But the ecosystem around them has grown more complex, and expertise now also needs to be discoverable.

Being visible online doesn't mean becoming an influencer, speaking more loudly, or turning medicine into a consumer product. It means making your expertise understandable to the people who are already looking for it. Done well, a digital presence lets a doctor share a philosophy, demonstrate expertise and educate patients far beyond the walls of the practice.

> Visibility gives expertise somewhere to exist online.

#### Why Raisey Lab

Looking back, each chapter of my career taught me part of the same lesson. Google taught me performance. Luxury taught me perception. Aesthetic medicine taught me trust.

Raisey Lab sits where the three meet. It is not another social media agency, and its purpose is not to make clinics "look better" online. It works on the whole presence around a practice: brand, digital experience, acquisition, content, patient journey and growth.

The strongest practices need more than visibility. They need to be understood and trusted, and when the right patient starts looking, they need to be there.

**Title:** Your Patient Has Already Met You Before Entering Your Clinic

**Standfirst:** The patient journey no longer starts at the clinic. For many patients, it starts with a search.

Think about the last restaurant you chose. Perhaps someone recommended it; perhaps you simply needed somewhere for dinner. Either way, you probably didn't just walk in. You searched, looked at the rating, read a few reviews, checked the photos. Maybe you went to Instagram to get a feel for the place, or to TikTok to see what other people were saying. Without quite realising it, you had formed an opinion before you ever stepped through the door.

We do this with restaurants, hotels and spas. More and more, people do it with aesthetic medicine too.

#### The decision has moved online

The journey used to feel fairly linear: a recommendation, an appointment, an experience. Now there are many small moments between hearing a name and making a decision.

A recommendation may still start it. Then might come a search, a Google Business Profile, the website, a few reviews, an interview or an educational video, a return to Instagram, another search, and only then, perhaps, a booking. Not every patient takes every step, and no two take them in the same order.

The shift isn't just that people spend more time online. Digital platforms have become part of the decision itself.

#### There is no single patient journey

A common mistake is to assume that everyone discovers you in the same place. They don't.

One doctor's audience may watch long-form educational content on YouTube. Another's may find them mainly through Instagram. Some patients first come across a treatment or a technique on TikTok, long before they search for a practitioner. Someone else types "aesthetic doctor near me" into Google Maps and begins there.

There are many journeys, not one. So digital strategy shouldn't begin with "We need to post more on Instagram." It should begin with three questions.

**Who is your patient? Where do they search? What do they need in order to trust you?**

#### Each platform plays a different role

Being present everywhere doesn't mean doing the same thing everywhere. Broadly, TikTok can create discovery. Instagram can build familiarity and desire. Google captures intent, the moment someone is actively looking. A Google Business Profile adds reassurance through reviews, ratings, photographs and practical details. YouTube can build depth and authority. The website is where it all has to make sense.

These are tendencies, not rules, and they shift from one audience and market to another. But no platform replaces the others. Together, they form an ecosystem.

#### Every touchpoint reduces uncertainty, or adds to it

Imagine discovering a doctor through a TikTok video. You like the way she explains things, so you look her up on Instagram, and the content feels coherent. You search her name and the reviews reassure you. On the website the treatments are clearly explained, the visual identity feels considered, and booking is simple. Each interaction confirms the one before.

Now imagine the same journey with a single break in it: an outdated website, no recent reviews, a feed that says something different from the videos. Doubt enters, and the search continues, often somewhere else.

> Each touchpoint reduces uncertainty, or adds to it.

#### The relationship begins before the appointment

The relationship between a doctor and a patient no longer necessarily begins when they meet. A prospective patient may have watched the doctor explain the very concern she has. She may have read about [their approach to natural results](/insights/aesthetic-medicine-skin-quality-language/), seen how they answer questions, understood why they favour one approach over another. She may have followed them quietly for six months.

So when she finally books, she isn't meeting a complete stranger. A relationship has already begun. It isn't a medical relationship yet, but it is a relationship with the doctor's expertise, [point of view and presence](/insights/why-i-created-raisey-lab/).

#### Don't be everywhere. Be where your patients are.

A doctor doesn't need TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, LinkedIn and whatever platform appears tomorrow. Presence without purpose creates noise.

The three questions do the work here. *Who is your patient?* Think about her concerns, her stage of life and [her relationship with aesthetic treatment](/insights/international-aesthetic-medicine-culture/). *Where does she search,* and what is she asking when she does? *What does she need in order to trust you?* That means knowing what reassures her, what makes her hesitate, which content helps her understand your approach, and which touchpoints bring her closer to an appointment.

The answers differ from one practice to the next. A dermatologist whose patients research a skin condition in depth needs a different presence from a practice whose patients discover treatments through short video. The aim isn't to be everywhere. It is to have the right presence, in the right places, for the right people.

#### The clinic starts before the clinic

For aesthetic practices, the digital experience has become an extension of the physical one. Your Google Business Profile isn't just a listing, your website isn't just a brochure, and your Instagram isn't just a feed. Together they form the first chapters of the patient experience.

Long before someone sits in your consultation room, those chapters are already answering her questions. Do I understand this doctor? Do I trust their approach? Is this for someone like me? Would I feel comfortable here?

By the time she walks through the door, much of the decision may already have been made.

> Your patients are already making decisions online, whether or not you take part in that journey.

**Title:** Beauty Has No Passport

**Standfirst:** Why aesthetic medicine cannot communicate the same way everywhere.

Beauty has never been universal. What we consider beautiful changes with time, with age and with culture, and it often changes when we cross a border.

Yet in an increasingly global aesthetic medicine industry, it is tempting to assume that the same treatment, the same imagery and the same communication strategy can simply travel from Paris to London, Dubai, Seoul or São Paulo. They can't. Behind every aesthetic trend sits something more complex, a culture of beauty, and understanding that culture has become part of understanding the patient.

#### We don't all mean the same thing by "beautiful"

What feels sophisticated in Paris may read as understated elsewhere. What one patient in Dubai finds desirable may not be what another in London is looking for. And no city has a single definition of beauty. Age, background, community, generation, gender and digital culture all play a part.

So this is not about national stereotypes. It is about the different relationships cultures have with beauty and with aesthetic intervention. These tendencies are worth understanding precisely because no individual patient is bound by them. Culture matters, but stereotypes are not strategy.

#### France: the art of not looking done

One strong current in French aesthetic culture celebrates restraint. The hoped-for reaction is often not "Look what I had done" but "You look well. What's changed?" For some patients, the ideal treatment is precisely the one nobody can identify.

That doesn't mean every patient in France wants the same thing. But it shows something important: sometimes the aspiration isn't transformation. It is imperceptibility. Communication is shaped locally too. French doctors work under professional rules that separate information from advertising, and those rules shape what a practice's digital presence can say.

#### London: many references in one waiting room

London is among the most international cities in the world. A single practice may see patients from many cultural backgrounds in the same week, each carrying different references for what "natural" means and how visible a result should be. Here, local intelligence means recognising the several markets inside one city.

#### The Gulf: several aesthetic cultures at once

In parts of the Gulf market, aesthetic treatment is discussed with less reticence than in some European markets, and social media plays a visible role in shaping demand. Dubai adds another dimension. It is an extraordinarily international city, where most residents were born elsewhere.

Rather than speaking of one "Dubai beauty ideal", it is more accurate to think of several aesthetic cultures existing side by side. Facial treatments coexist with body aesthetics. A preference for subtle rejuvenation coexists with a taste for more visible enhancement, sometimes in the same clinic on the same day.

Localisation isn't translation. It is understanding aspiration.

#### Turkey: when the patient is also a traveller

In Turkey, aesthetic medicine sits alongside a large international medical-tourism industry. Many patients are local, but others travel specifically for a procedure.

That changes the digital journey. A patient choosing a clinic in another country usually cannot visit it before deciding. So [the website, social content, reviews, practitioner credentials, before-and-after documentation and consultation process carry even more weight](/insights/aesthetic-medicine-patient-journey/). So does a clear plan for aftercare once she has flown home.

#### South Korea: beauty as an ecosystem

In South Korea, beauty cannot be reduced to cosmetic surgery. Skincare, cosmetics, technology, aesthetic treatments, surgery, celebrity culture and digital media form one connected ecosystem. Even international demand reflects this. Korea received a record 1.17 million foreign patients in 2024. According to the Ministry of Health and Welfare, [56.6% came for dermatology](/insights/aesthetic-medicine-skin-quality-language/) and 11.4% for plastic surgery.

Even so, it would be a mistake to speak of a single "Asian beauty standard". Seoul is not Tokyo, Tokyo is not Bangkok, and Bangkok is not Shanghai. Younger generations don't necessarily share the aspirations of their parents. A market is a living culture.

#### Brazil: when the body enters the conversation

Brazil shows clearly why a global aesthetic strategy cannot focus on the face alone. It is one of the world's largest aesthetic markets. In the ISAPS global survey for 2024 it ranked second only to the United States, with around 3.1 million procedures, and body procedures hold a significant place in its aesthetic landscape. The categories change, the aspirations change, and so the conversation changes.

#### A global brand needs local intelligence

Imagine an aesthetic medicine brand expanding internationally. The easiest approach is to create one campaign, translate it, change the location and launch. But translation doesn't create relevance.

The same before-and-after imagery may not provoke the same response. The same treatment benefit may not be the main motivation. Even the words can carry different meanings from one market to the next: natural, enhancement, rejuvenation, anti-ageing, contouring. The rules on what a clinic may show and say differ as well.

So before asking "What should we post?", ask "[What does beauty mean to this audience?](/insights/why-i-created-raisey-lab/)"

#### Beauty is global. Desire remains local.

Global trends exist, and social media helps them travel faster than ever. But globalisation doesn't erase culture; it interacts with it. A trend arrives somewhere and is reinterpreted, adapted, sometimes rejected, sometimes transformed entirely.

Paris, London, Dubai, Istanbul, Seoul and São Paulo may all be talking about beauty. They are not necessarily having the same conversation, and understanding the difference is where strategy begins.

**Title:** We Used to Talk About Volume. Now We Talk About Skin.

**Standfirst:** How the language of aesthetic medicine is changing, and what it says about the way we want to age.

There was a time when aesthetic medicine spoke a very different language: wrinkles, volume, fillers, correction, anti-ageing. The objective seemed straightforward. Identify what ageing had changed, and correct it. A wrinkle appeared; we softened it. Volume was lost; we replaced it. A contour changed; we restored it.

Then, slowly, the vocabulary began to change. Scroll through the websites and social feeds of many aesthetic practices today and you will meet a different set of words: skin quality, glow, collagen, biostimulation, regeneration, prevention, longevity.

This isn't just a copywriting trend. It reflects a broader shift in how parts of aesthetic medicine think about treatment, and in what many patients hope to gain from it.

#### From correction to preservation

For years, aesthetic medicine was communicated mainly as the correction of visible ageing. Those treatments haven't gone anywhere. Botulinum toxin and hyaluronic acid fillers remain the two most performed non-surgical procedures in the world.

What has changed is the conversation around them. Alongside "What can we add?", a second question has become common: "What can we preserve, stimulate or improve?" That is a different philosophy of treatment, even when the tools overlap.

#### Skin quality became an objective in its own right

One of the most interesting changes is the rise of skin quality as a category of its own. In 2021, an international panel of experts proposed describing it through four perceptual dimensions: tone evenness, surface evenness, firmness and glow. They meant the framework to apply across ages, ethnicities and genders.

The language matters because patients and practitioners don't always use the same words for the same concern. A patient says, "I want glow." A doctor may see hydration, texture, pigmentation, laxity or something else entirely. They are looking at the same face but not always speaking the same language, and [part of a doctor's digital presence is building a bridge between the two](/insights/aesthetic-medicine-patient-journey/).

#### Regeneration: a powerful word that needs care

"Regenerative" may be one of the most powerful words in contemporary aesthetic medicine. It is also one of the words that deserves the most caution.

As a scientific idea, regenerative aesthetics is less about changing appearance directly and more about supporting or restoring aspects of tissue structure and function. Some treatments, such as collagen biostimulators, are designed to prompt the body's own collagen production, and there is clinical research behind several of them. Other interventions marketed under the same banner rest on far thinner evidence, including exosomes, stem-cell-derived products and some peptides. A 2024 systematic review in *Aesthetic Plastic Surgery* found a significant lack of consistent, high-quality evidence for many of them.

So not everything described as "regenerative" stands on the same ground. A fashionable word should never replace scientific precision. For a doctor communicating online, being clear about what a treatment can and cannot do is itself a mark of credibility.

#### From anti-ageing to ageing well

For decades, beauty marketed itself through opposition: anti-age, anti-wrinkle, correct, erase, reverse. Ageing was something to fight.

Today the vocabulary speaks more often of ageing well: maintenance, skin health, long-term treatment plans and, lately, longevity. That last word is borrowed from medicine and wellness, and it is worth using carefully. The shift changes the promise. The fantasy is no longer necessarily to become younger. It can simply be to remain yourself for longer.

#### Prevention, and the question of when

This philosophy opens another conversation: prevention. Some practitioners favour beginning certain interventions earlier, before visible ageing becomes established. Others take a more conservative view and treat only when there is a clear individual indication. The debate is ongoing, and there is no universal age at which anyone "should" begin aesthetic treatment.

Two thirty-year-olds don't have the same face. Two fifty-year-olds don't age the same way. Their anatomy, skin, history, lifestyle and expectations all differ. Age alone cannot define a treatment plan.

#### Every doctor has a philosophy of beauty

Give the same patient to five practitioners and they may not propose the same approach. One may prioritise skin quality, another structural support, and a third may recommend treating nothing at all. Aesthetic medicine is not only a collection of products and techniques. It involves interpretation, restraint, proportion and an understanding of how faces age. In short, it involves [a philosophy of beauty](/insights/why-i-created-raisey-lab/).

That philosophy is one of the most powerful things a doctor can communicate. Not "These are the treatments I perform," but "This is how I see a face." When treatment menus look alike, the philosophy is what tells one practitioner from another, and it deserves a place at the centre of a doctor's digital identity.

Sometimes that philosophy includes the confidence to recommend less, or nothing at all. That idea deserves its own essay, and it is the subject of the next Insight.

#### The vocabulary changed because the aspiration changed

Volume hasn't disappeared. Neither have fillers, botulinum toxin or correction. They have been joined by another vocabulary: quality, preservation, stimulation, regeneration, prevention, longevity.

Language is rarely accidental. It tells us what an industry values and what patients are asking for. Above all, it tells us something about our changing relationship with beauty.

> We used to ask: how can I look younger? More often now, the question is: how can I age well, and still look like myself?
